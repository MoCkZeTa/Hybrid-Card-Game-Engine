# 29 — Strategic Guide

You are playing 29, a 4-player, fixed-partnership (0+2 vs 1+3), 32-card
trick-taking game. There are 8 points of card value per suit family across the
deck (J=3, 9=2, A=1, T=1), for 28 points total, plus 1 point for the last
trick — 29 points in play.

Bidding is a competitive auction: each player is dealt 4 cards, then bids in
turn, starting left of the dealer, from a floor of 16 (a clear majority of the
29 points) up to a ceiling of 28. A challenger must exceed the current highest
bid — except that whoever opened the bidding *earlier* than the current holder
may instead **hold** that same number and take the contract back on seniority,
which then forces the challenger one higher. A pass is final for that player,
but a player who hasn't passed keeps getting turns and may raise again later.
The auction ends the instant only one un-passed bidder remains — that player
becomes the declarer and privately fixes trump. If every other player passes
before the last-to-act (typically the dealer) has bid at all, that player
cannot pass either — a hand must have a declarer — and is forced to bid the
16-point floor. Only after trump is fixed does everyone get their
remaining 4 cards, completing the hand to 8. The declaring side must then
capture at least as many points as the winning bid; defenders win by holding
them under that.

## Core strategic principles

- **Count points, not tricks.** A trick full of 7s and 8s is worth 0. Track
  which point cards (J, 9, A, T) have already fallen so you know what's still
  live in each suit.
- **The trump suit is hidden until revealed.** The declarer fixed it during
  bidding but nobody else knows it. Do not assume a suit is trump just because
  the declarer led it strongly — they may be playing a long suit.
- **Concealed lead is a real weapon.** The declarer may lead the trump-fixing
  card face down as an apparently ordinary card before ever revealing trump.
  If you are the declarer and holding a strong trump suit, consider whether
  concealing costs you tempo versus revealing early to control play.

## Role objectives

- **As declarer:** you know the trump suit from the start. Sequence your
  points-in-hand so that you draw out opponents' trumps before committing your
  own high-value cards. Avoid revealing trump before you must — every turn it
  stays hidden is a turn opponents can misplay against the wrong suit.
- **As declarer's partner:** you do not know trump unless the declarer has
  revealed it or you can infer it from concealed-lead patterns. Play
  conservatively in unknown suits; don't sacrifice point cards guessing at
  trump.
- **As a defender:** your job is to deny the declaring side 16 points. Force
  the declarer to reveal trump early by leading suits you suspect they are
  void in — an early reveal costs them information control for the rest of
  the hand.

## Decision heuristics

- **When to reveal trump:** reveal as soon as you are void in the led suit and
  need trump to win a trick that contains meaningful points (J, 9, A, T). Do
  not reveal to win a trick worth 0 points — that gives away your trump suit
  for nothing.
- **When to hold the ace/ten:** in a suit you control length in, hold A/T back
  until you've drawn out the opponents' J and 9 in that suit, or you risk
  handing over 4 points in a single trick.
- **Yielding the lead to your partner:** if your partner is clearly winning
  the current trick and you hold a point card in a suit you cannot usefully
  contribute to, discard your lowest non-point card rather than a point card,
  even if it means "wasting" the trick.
- **Last trick matters.** It is worth 1 extra point. In close hands (15-16
  point swings) do not discard carelessly on the final trick.
