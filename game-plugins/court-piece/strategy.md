# Court Piece (Rung) — Strategic Guide

You are playing Court Piece, a 4-player partnership trick-taking game on a
full 52-card deck. You and the player across from you are a team. Everyone
gets 13 cards and 13 tricks are played.

**Trump is set by the opening lead.** Whoever leads the very first card of
the hand fixes that card's suit as trump for the whole hand — there is no
bidding and no separate trump-naming step. This makes the opening lead the
single most consequential decision in the game.

**Scoring rewards the margin, not the win.** Your side scores the tricks it
takes above six, so seven tricks is worth one and thirteen is worth seven.
Both sides can score zero in the same hand; only the side that clears seven
banks anything.

## The opening lead

If you are on lead for the first trick, you are choosing trump. Choose the
suit where you are longest, not the one where you are highest — length wins
more tricks than raw rank, because a long trump suit lets you take control
after drawing the opposition's trumps out. Five or more cards in a suit is a
strong trump choice; four with two honours is workable; three is a gamble
that hands the hand to the opposition.

Remember your partner's hand is unknown to you, so pick the suit you can win
with unaided.

## Core strategic principles

- **Draw trump when you are long in it.** If you and your partner clearly
  hold the trump length, lead trump early and repeatedly. Once the opponents
  are void of trump, your side-suit winners become unstoppable.
- **Do not draw trump when you are short.** If trump was set by an opponent
  and you hold two or fewer, your trumps are worth more as ruffing tools.
  Force the long-trump side to spend theirs by leading your own long suit.
- **Count to seven, then to more.** The first seven tricks are worth
  nothing until they are all secured, so early play should be about locking
  in the majority. Once seven is safe, every extra trick is a full point and
  is worth taking risks for.
- **Partnership signalling through discards.** When you cannot follow suit,
  the card you throw tells your partner where your strength is. Discard from
  your weakest suit consistently so your partner can read it.

## Decision heuristics

- **Leading (after the first trick):** lead your long side suit to force
  ruffs, or lead trump if your side controls it. Avoid leading a suit where
  you hold an unsupported King or Queen.
- **Following when you can:** third hand plays high to win the trick;
  second hand plays low unless the trick is already valuable. If your
  partner is winning, throw your lowest card.
- **Following when you cannot:** trumping is optional here, not required.
  Ruff when the trick is contested or when your side still needs tricks
  toward seven; discard your weakest card when your partner already has the
  trick won.
- **Endgame:** by trick nine you should know the exact trump count remaining
  and which suits are exhausted. If your side has already cleared seven,
  play every remaining trick for the extra point.
