# Oh Hell — Strategic Guide

You are playing Oh Hell, a game where every player is on their own. It seats
3 to 7 players and is played with a standard 52-card deck.

**The hand size changes every round.** The match deals 7 cards, then 6, then
5, down to a single card, then climbs back up to 7 — thirteen hands in all.
A one-card hand and a seven-card hand are almost different games, and your
approach has to change with them.

**Trump is turned up, not chosen.** After the deal, the top card of the
undealt stock is turned face up and its suit is trump for that hand. Nobody
bids for it and nobody selects it.

## The bid is exact — that is the whole game

Before play, every seat bids exactly how many tricks it will take.

- **Hit your bid exactly:** 10 points, plus 1 per trick taken.
- **Miss it in either direction:** 0 points.

Taking *more* tricks than you bid is just as bad as taking fewer. Bidding 2
and taking 4 scores nothing at all. This inverts the instinct from every
other trick-taking game: much of your play will be about deliberately
*losing* tricks once your bid is filled.

**Bidding zero is a real strategy**, not a surrender — take no tricks at all
and you score 10. On small hands it is often the safest bid available.

## The hook rule

The last player to bid may not choose the number that would make the table's
bids add up to exactly the number of tricks available. Someone always has to
miss. This has two consequences:

- If you bid early, you have more freedom but less information.
- If you bid last, you are constrained, so pay close attention to whether
  the table is collectively over- or under-bid. If the bids so far total
  *less* than the tricks available, spare tricks are floating around and
  your own bid is easier to make than it looks.

## Bidding by hand size

- **One and two cards:** almost pure luck. Count only certainties — a lone
  Ace or a trump. Bid 0 unless you hold something that clearly wins.
- **Three to five cards:** count trumps and Aces. Each is roughly one trick.
  Small hands punish optimism; round down.
- **Six and seven cards:** now shape matters. A void or singleton in a side
  suit plus two or more trumps generates extra tricks through ruffing. Long
  trump holdings are worth more than raw high cards.

## Core strategic principles

- **Know which way you need to steer.** At every point in the hand you are
  either still chasing tricks or actively dodging them. These call for
  opposite plays, and confusing them is the most common way to lose a hand.
- **Fill your bid early when chasing.** Cash your certain winners while
  trumps are still out and your Aces are safe. Waiting risks them being
  ruffed away.
- **Dodge late when ducking.** Once your bid is complete, play your highest
  dangerous cards on tricks that are already lost, so you are not stranded
  holding a winner at the end with nobody left to take it.
- **Watch the other bids.** Add them up. If the table is under-bid, extra
  tricks have to land somewhere and you may be forced to take one you did
  not want; play your escapes earlier. If it is over-bid, tricks are scarce
  and yours may not come — fight for them sooner.

## Decision heuristics

- **Leading while chasing:** lead trumps if you hold the top ones, or lead
  Aces in side suits before they can be ruffed.
- **Leading while ducking:** lead your lowest card in the longest suit still
  held by others. Never lead trump.
- **Following while chasing:** win as cheaply as you can — save the big cards
  for later tricks you still need.
- **Following while ducking:** play the highest card that still loses. If you
  are forced to win, get it over with early rather than at the end.
- **Endgame:** in the last two or three tricks, count exactly what is left
  outstanding. The difference between hitting and missing an exact bid is
  usually a single correctly-timed duck.
