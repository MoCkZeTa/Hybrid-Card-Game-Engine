# Spades — Strategic Guide

You are playing Spades, a 4-player partnership game. You and the player
across from you are a team; your scores are combined. Everyone gets 13 cards
from a full 52-card deck and 13 tricks are played.

**Spades are always trump.** There is no trump selection — the suit is fixed
before the deal. Spades may not be *led* until a Spade has already been
played on an earlier trick ("spades broken"), though you may always ruff with
one when void in the suit led.

## How scoring works, and why it is unusual

Before play, every seat bids the number of tricks it expects to take. Your
side's contract is the two bids added together.

- **Make the contract:** 10 points per trick bid, plus 1 point for each
  trick taken beyond it.
- **Miss the contract:** minus 10 points per trick bid. Missing by one costs
  exactly as much as missing by ten.
- **Bags:** each overtrick is worth only 1 point now but accumulates. Every
  10 accumulated overtricks costs your side **100 points**. This is the
  trap that decides most matches.
- **Nil:** a bid of 0 is a separate contract — take no tricks at all and
  your side gains 100; take even one and it loses 100.

The match runs to 500 points.

## Bidding

Count your near-certain tricks, not your hopeful ones:

- Ace of Spades and King of Spades are tricks. So is the Queen if you hold
  three or more Spades to protect her.
- Every Spade beyond the third is roughly half a trick, because length ruffs.
- An Ace in a side suit is a trick. A King is a trick only if you hold a
  second card in that suit.
- Voids and singletons in side suits are worth tricks *only* if you hold
  enough Spades to ruff with.

**Bid slightly under, never over.** An underbid costs you a bag; an overbid
costs you the whole contract. But do not underbid habitually either — bags
compound into a 100-point disaster.

### When to bid nil

Nil needs a hand with no high cards and no forced winners: at most three low
Spades (nothing above a 5 or so), no Aces, and no unprotected Kings. A
singleton King is fatal. Your partner should bid a little higher than normal
to cover you, since they will be taking tricks for both of you.

## Core strategic principles

- **Bags are the real enemy.** A side that makes every contract but collects
  a bag a hand loses 100 points every ten hands. Once your contract is safe,
  actively *avoid* winning further tricks — duck, discard high, let the
  opponents have them.
- **Count trump.** Thirteen Spades exist. Track them. Knowing when the
  opponents are void of trump tells you exactly when your side-suit winners
  are safe to cash.
- **Draw trump when your side is long, ruff when it is short.** If you and
  your partner bid heavily on Spade length, lead Spades to strip the
  opponents of ruffing power. If you are short, keep your Spades to ruff
  their side-suit winners.
- **Protect a nil bidder.** If your partner bid nil, take every trick you
  possibly can — win with your high cards early, lead suits they are likely
  void in last, and never leave them on lead.

## Decision heuristics

- **Leading:** lead side-suit Aces early while they are still safe from
  ruffs. Do not lead Spades unless your side is long in them or you are
  hunting the opponents' trump.
- **Following when you can:** win cheaply if you still need tricks; play
  your lowest if your contract is already secure or your partner has the
  trick.
- **Following when you cannot:** ruff only if you need the trick. If your
  contract is already made, discard instead — that ruff would become a bag.
- **Attacking a nil:** if an opponent bid nil, lead low cards in suits where
  they may be forced to win. Making them take a single trick is worth 100
  points.
- **Endgame:** in the last few tricks, count precisely what your side still
  needs. Deliberately losing a winnable trick to avoid a bag is a good play,
  and one that inexperienced players never make.
